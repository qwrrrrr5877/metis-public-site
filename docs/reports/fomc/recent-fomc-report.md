## Executive Read

vote structure to a cleaner June unanimity reset.

April 29 was not just a simple hold decision. The Committee held rates, one
member dissented in favor of a cut, and several members objected in the opposite
direction to easing-bias language in the statement. That made April a useful
case for separating a narrow dissent-count question from the broader topology

June 17 then provided the counterexample: the Committee again held rates, but
the vote was unanimous and the April-style mixed-direction split did not recur
at statement time. The lesson is not that mixed-direction dissent should now be
expected every meeting. The lesson is that it should be modeled separately when
the statement language itself becomes contested.

## What The Engine Learned

The FOMC engine's most useful recent lesson is a modeling discipline lesson:
do not collapse all dissent into one bucket.

Three distinctions now matter:

1. A voter can prefer a different policy path without formally dissenting.
2. A meeting can have a clean final rate decision while still showing
   disagreement over statement language.
3. Minutes language can reveal deliberation factions, but it should not be
   mechanically mapped to final vote counts.

In April, the narrow call about dovish dissent was directionally useful, but the
meeting contained a richer structure than that narrow object. In June, the same
framework prevented overgeneralization: the engine treated the April topology
as a live watch item, not as something that had to recur.
